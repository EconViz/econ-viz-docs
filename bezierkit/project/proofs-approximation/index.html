    Approximation proofs

    Cubic segments and paths

    Proof

    Bounding box of a cubic

    Multiplying out the cubic Bernstein form gives

    \[ \begin{aligned}B(t) &= (1-t)^3 P_0 + 3t(1-t)^2 P_1 + 3t^2(1-t) P_2 + t^3 P_3 \\ &= (1 - 3t + 3t^2 - t^3) P_0 + (3t - 6t^2 + 3t^3) P_1 + (3t^2 - 3t^3) P_2 + t^3 P_3 \\ &= a t^3 + b t^2 + c t + P_0,\end{aligned} \]

    with \(a\), \(b\) and \(c\) as stated, by collecting the powers of \(t\).

    Fix a coordinate \(k\). The function \(B_k\) is a polynomial, hence continuous on the compact interval \([0, 1]\), so it attains a minimum and a maximum there. Let \(t^*\) be a point where it attains one of them. If \(t^* \in (0, 1)\), then \(B_k\) is differentiable with an extremum at an interior point, so \(B'_k (t^*) = 0\) (Fermat's theorem). Two cases remain. - \(B'_k\) is not identically zero. It is a polynomial of degree at most 2, so it has at most two roots, and \(t^*\) is one of them if \(t^*\) lies in \((0, 1)\). In every case \(t^* \in T_k\). - \(B'_k\) is identically zero. Then \(B_k\) is constant, so its extrema are attained at \(t = 0\) as well, and \(0 \in T_k\). Therefore the minimum and the maximum of \(B_k\) over \([0, 1]\) equal those over the finite set \(T_k \subset [0, 1]\), which are \(\alpha_k\) and \(\beta_k\) as stated, since they are the infimum and supremum of \(B_k\) over the curve. By Axis-aligned bounding box, the bounding box is the product of the intervals \([\alpha_k, \beta_k]\): a box that contains the curve contains every value \(B_k (t)\), hence \(\alpha_k\) and \(\beta_k\), and the product of these intervals contains the curve.

    Proof

    Lines and quadratics as cubics

    Line. The line \(t |\to (1 - t) P_0 + t P_3\) is a Bézier curve of degree 1 with controls \(R_0 = P_0\) and \(R_1 = P_3\). By Degree elevation with \(n = 1\), it is the quadratic with controls

    \[ Q_0 = P_0, \quad Q_1 = 1/2 P_0 + 1/2 P_3, \quad Q_2 = P_3. \]

    Applying Degree elevation again, with \(n = 2\), gives the cubic with controls

    \[ \begin{aligned}S_0 &= Q_0 = P_0, \\ S_1 &= 1/3 Q_0 + 2/3 Q_1 = 2/3 P_0 + 1/3 P_3, \\ S_2 &= 2/3 Q_1 + 1/3 Q_2 = 1/3 P_0 + 2/3 P_3, \\ S_3 &= Q_2 = P_3,\end{aligned} \]

    and \(S_i = P_0 + i/3 (P_3 - P_0)\) for \(i = 0, \ldots, 3\).

    Quadratic. By Degree elevation with \(n = 2\) and controls \(P_0, C, P_3\), the cubic controls are

    \[ Q_0 = P_0, \quad Q_1 = 1/3 P_0 + 2/3 C, \quad Q_2 = 2/3 C + 1/3 P_3, \quad Q_3 = P_3, \]

    and \(Q_1 = P_0 + 2/3 (C - P_0)\), \(Q_2 = P_3 + 2/3 (C - P_3)\).

    Each step is an identity in \(t\), so the curve and its parameterization are unchanged.

    Proof

    Continuity of cubic joins

    Write \(B_S\) and \(B_T\) for the cubic Bézier curves with controls \(P_i\) and \(Q_i\), so that \(S(u) = B_S ((u - u_0 + h_S) / h_S)\) and \(T(u) = B_T ((u - u_0) / h_T)\). At \(u = u_0\) the arguments are \(1\) and \(0\). By Endpoints, \(S(u_0) = P_3\) and \(T(u_0) = Q_0\). By the chain rule and End tangents,

    \[ S'(u_0) = (B'_S (1)) / h_S = (3(P_3 - P_2)) / h_S, \quad T'(u_0) = (B'_T (0)) / h_T = (3(Q_1 - Q_0)) / h_T. \]
    • By Parametric continuity with \(k = 0\), \(C\) is \(C^0\) at \(u_0\) if and only if \(S(u_0) = T(u_0)\), that is \(P_3 = Q_0\).
    • With \(P_3 = Q_0\), Parametric continuity with \(k = 1\) adds \(S'(u_0) = T'(u_0)\), which is the stated equation after division by 3.
    • With \(P_3 = Q_0\), by Geometric continuity \(G^1\) the join is \(G^1\) if and only if \(S'(u_0) \ne 0\), \(T'(u_0) \ne 0\), and \(T'(u_0) = \lambda S'(u_0)\) for some \(\lambda > 0\). The two tangents are non-zero if and only if \(P_3 \ne P_2\) and \(Q_1 \ne Q_0\), and then
    \[ Q_1 - Q_0 = (\lambda h_T / h_S) (P_3 - P_2). \]

    Conversely, if \(Q_1 - Q_0 = \mu (P_3 - P_2)\) with \(\mu > 0\), then \(\lambda = \mu h_S / h_T > 0\) gives \(T'(u_0) = \lambda S'(u_0)\). + A path of \(m\) segments under the uniform parameterization gives each segment the span \(1 / m\), so \(h_S = h_T\) and the equation in 2 reduces to \(P_3 - P_2 = Q_1 - Q_0\).

    Constructions and interpolation

    Proof

    Endpoint conditions

    For the curve \(B\) with the stated controls, Endpoints gives \(B(0) = P_0\) and \(B(1) = P_3\), and End tangents gives

    \[ B'(0) = 3(P_1 - P_0) = D_0, \quad B'(1) = 3(P_3 - P_2) = D_1. \]

    Now let \(\Gamma\) be any polynomial curve of degree at most 3 with \(\Gamma(0) = P_0\), \(\Gamma(1) = P_3\), \(\Gamma'(0) = D_0\) and \(\Gamma'(1) = D_1\). By Bernstein basis, applied to each coordinate, there are unique \(R_0, \ldots, R_3\) with \(\Gamma(t) = \sum_i b_{i,3}(t) R_i\). The computation above, applied to the controls \(R_i\), shows

    \[ \Gamma(0) = R_0, \quad \Gamma(1) = R_3, \quad \Gamma'(0) = 3(R_1 - R_0), \quad \Gamma'(1) = 3(R_3 - R_2). \]

    Comparing with the four conditions gives \(R_0 = P_0\), \(R_3 = P_3\), \(R_1 = P_0 + D_0 / 3\) and \(R_2 = P_3 - D_1 / 3\). So \(R_i\) are the stated controls and \(\Gamma = B\).

    Proof

    Existence and uniqueness

    Put \(G(s) = H(x_0 + h s)\). The map \(H |\to G\) is a bijection of the polynomials of degree at most 3 onto themselves, with inverse \(G |\to G((x - x_0) / h)\). By the chain rule, \(H(x_i) = f_i\) and \(H'(x_i) = m_i\) for \(i = 0, 1\) are equivalent to

    \[ G(0) = f_0, \quad G(1) = f_1, \quad G'(0) = h m_0, \quad G'(1) = h m_1. \]

    By Endpoint conditions with \(d = 1\), \(P_0 = f_0\), \(P_3 = f_1\), \(D_0 = h m_0\) and \(D_1 = h m_1\), exactly one polynomial \(G\) of degree at most 3 satisfies these four conditions, and its Bernstein coefficients are \(c_0, \ldots, c_3\). Hence \(H\) exists, is unique, and equals \(\sum_i b_{i,3}((x - x_0) / h) c_i\).

    Proof

    Hermite interpolation

    At \(t = t_0\) and \(t = t_1\) the segment is evaluated at \(s = 0\) and \(s = 1\), so \(H(t_0) = P_0\) and \(H(t_1) = P_3\) by Endpoints. By the chain rule \(H'(t) = B'(s) / h\). By End tangents, \(B'(0) = 3(P_1 - P_0) = h D_0\) and \(B'(1) = 3(P_3 - P_2) = h D_1\), so \(H'(t_0) = D_0\) and \(H'(t_1) = D_1\). Nothing requires \(h > 0\).

    Proof

    Graph form

    Write the segment as \((X(s), Y(s))\). The controls of \(X\) are

    \[ x_0, \quad x_0 + h/3, \quad x_1 - h/3 = x_0 + 2h/3, \quad x_1, \]

    which by Lines and quadratics as cubics (the line from \(x_0\) to \(x_1\)) are those of the line \(s |\to x_0 + h s\). Hence \(X(s) = x_0 + h s\). The controls of \(Y\) are \(y_0, y_0 + h m_0 / 3, y_1 - h m_1 / 3, y_1\), which are the coefficients \(c_0, \ldots, c_3\) of Existence and uniqueness for the data \(y_i = f(x_i)\) and \(m_i = f'(x_i)\). Hence \(Y(s) = H(x_0 + h s)\), and the segment is \(s |\to (x_0 + h s, H(x_0 + h s))\).

    Proof

    Hermite error bound

    The case \(x \in \lbrace x_0, x_1\rbrace\) is trivial: both sides of the first formula vanish, for any \(\xi\). Let \(x_0 < x < x_1\), and put

    \[ E = f - H, \quad w(t) = (t - x_0)^2 (t - x_1)^2, \quad K = E(x) / w(x), \quad g = E - K w. \]

    Then \(w(x) > 0\), \(g\) is four times continuously differentiable, and \(g(x_0) = g(x) = g(x_1) = 0\), because \(E\) and \(w\) vanish at \(x_0\) and \(x_1\), and \(g(x) = E(x) - K w(x) = 0\). Moreover \(g'(x_0) = g'(x_1) = 0\): \(E'(x_i) = f'(x_i) - H'(x_i) = 0\) by Cubic Hermite interpolant, and \(w\) has a double root at each \(x_i\), so \(w'(x_i) = 0\).

    By Rolle's theorem (Theorem 1.7 of Burden (2011)), \(g'\) vanishes at some \(\xi_1 \in (x_0, x)\) and at some \(\xi_2 \in (x, x_1)\). So \(g'\) has the four distinct zeros \(x_0 < \xi_1 < \xi_2 < x_1\). The function \(g'\) is continuous and three times differentiable, so by the generalized Rolle theorem (Theorem 1.10 of Burden (2011)) there is a \(\xi \in (x_0, x_1)\) with \(g^{(4)}(\xi) = 0\). Since \(H\) has degree at most 3 and \(w\) is a monic polynomial of degree 4, \(H^{(4)} = 0\) and \(w^{(4)} = 24\), so

    \[ 0 = g^{(4)}(\xi) = f^{(4)}(\xi) - 24 K, \quad \text{that is} \quad K = (f^{(4)}(\xi)) / 24. \]

    Hence \(f(x) - H(x) = E(x) = K w(x)\), which is the first formula.

    For the bound, \((x - x_0)(x_1 - x)\) is a product of two non-negative numbers with the sum \(h\), so it is at most \((h / 2)^2\). Thus \(w(x) \le h^4 / 16\) and

    \[ |f(x) - H(x)| \le M / 24 \cdot h^4 / 16 = (M h^4) / 384. \]

    Fitting and level sets

    Proof

    Depth of adaptive fitting

    Let \([a, b]\) be an interval at depth \(k\), so \(b - a = L / 2^k\). On it, each coordinate of the Hermite segment is the cubic Hermite interpolant of the same coordinate of \(C\) (Hermite interpolation and Existence and uniqueness), so Hermite error bound bounds its error by \(M (b - a)^4 / 384\). The Euclidean error at any parameter is at most \(\sqrt{d}\) times the largest coordinate error, since \(\left\lVert v \right\rVert_2 \le \sqrt{d} \left\lVert v \right\rVert_\infty\) for \(v \in \mathbb{R}^d\): indeed \(\left\lVert v \right\rVert_2^2 = \sum_k v_k^2 \le d \left\lVert v \right\rVert_\infty^2\). In particular the measured error, a maximum over probe parameters, satisfies

    \[ e \le \sqrt{d} M L^4 / (384 \cdot 2^{4k}). \]

    This is at most \(\epsilon\) if and only if \(2^{4k} \ge \sqrt{d} M L^4 / (384 \epsilon)\), that is, if \(k \ge 1/4 \log_2 (\sqrt{d} M L^4 / (384 \epsilon))\). Every depth \(k \ge k^*\) satisfies this, so every interval at such a depth is accepted.

    Suppose now that max_depth \(\ge k^*\) and max_segments \(\ge 2^{k^*}\). An interval is split only if it fails, which happens only at a depth \(k < k^*\). So all intervals have depth at most \(k^*\) and no ToleranceNotMet is raised for the depth. The accepted intervals are disjoint pieces obtained by halving \([t_0, t_1]\) at most \(k^*\) times, so there are at most \(2^{k^*}\) of them. A failing interval would give at least two accepted segments, so the number of segments accepted so far plus two never exceeds this final count, which is at most max_segments; the segment limit is therefore not exceeded either.

    Proof

    Polyline tolerance

    Let rdp\((i, j)\) denote the recursive step of the algorithm on the indices \(i < j\), which returns an increasing list of indices from \(i\) to \(j\). We show by strong induction on \(j - i\) that for every two consecutive indices \(u < v\) in the returned list, every index \(l\) with \(u \le l \le v\) satisfies \(\text{dist}(v_l, [v_u, v_v]) \le \epsilon\).

    Base case. If \(j \le i + 1\), the list is \((i, j)\), and the only indices between \(i\) and \(j\) inclusive are \(i\) and \(j\), whose vertices are the chord's end points, at distance zero.

    Step. Otherwise let \(\delta^*\) be the largest distance from a vertex strictly between \(i\) and \(j\) to \([v_i, v_j]\). If \(\delta^* \le \epsilon\), the list is \((i, j)\), and the claim holds by the choice of \(\delta^*\) and because the end points have distance zero. If \(\delta^* > \epsilon\), the step chooses an index \(m\) with \(i < m < j\) and returns the list of \(\text{rdp}(i, m)\) followed by that of \(\text{rdp}(m, j)\), with \(m\) occurring once. Two consecutive indices of the result are consecutive in one of the two lists. Since \(m - i\) and \(j - m\) are smaller than \(j - i\), the induction hypothesis applies to both.

    Steps 2 and 3 apply rdp between every two consecutive kept vertices of step 2 and concatenate the results. The output keeps exactly the returned indices \(a_0 < \ldots < a_m\), and every two consecutive ones are consecutive in one of these lists. This proves the inequality. The fit_error of an output segment is \(\max_{a_j \le i \le a_{j+1}} \text{dist}(v_i, [v_{a_j}, v_{a_{j+1}}])\), hence at most \(\epsilon\).

    Proof

    Deviation from the polyline

    1. The distance to a convex set is a convex function. For the segment \(K = [a, b]\), let \(x_1, x_2\) be points with \(\text{dist}(x_i, K) \le \epsilon\), attained at \(y_i \in K\) (the minimum exists by compactness), and let \(\lambda \in [0, 1]\). Then \(\lambda y_1 + (1 - \lambda) y_2 \in K\) and by the triangle inequality
    \[ \left\lVert \lambda x_1 + (1 - \lambda) x_2 - (\lambda y_1 + (1 - \lambda) y_2) \right\rVert_2 \le \lambda \left\lVert x_1 - y_1 \right\rVert_2 + (1 - \lambda) \left\lVert x_2 - y_2 \right\rVert_2 \le \epsilon. \]

    So \(\text{dist}(\lambda x_1 + (1 - \lambda) x_2, K) \le \epsilon\).

    A point \(x\) of the polyline lies on an edge \([v_i, v_{i+1}]\), so it is \(\lambda v_i + (1 - \lambda) v_{i+1}\). Choose \(j\) with \(a_j \le i < a_{j+1}\); then \(i + 1 \le a_{j+1}\), since indices are integers, and both \(v_i\) and \(v_{i+1}\) are within \(\epsilon\) of \([v_{a_j}, v_{a_{j+1}}]\) by Polyline tolerance. By the convexity just proved, so is \(x\).

    1. A point left by step 1 is some \(v_i\), and \(v_i\) lies within \(\epsilon\) of a chord by Polyline tolerance. A dropped input point \(p\) is within \(\delta\) of the vertex kept before it, say \(v\); then \(\text{dist}(p, [a, b]) \le \left\lVert p - v \right\rVert_2 + \text{dist}(v, [a, b]) \le \delta + \epsilon\) for a chord \([a, b]\) within \(\epsilon\) of \(v\). The final copy of the first point of a closed polyline is dropped under the same condition, with \(v = v_0\).
    Proof

    Centre of the bilinear interpolant

    Substituting the four corners of \([0, 1]^2\) into \(u\) shows that only one of the four terms is non-zero at each corner, with the coefficient \(1\), so \(u\) takes the corner values. At \(s = t = 1 / 2\) each of the four products of weights equals \(1/2 \cdot 1/2 = 1/4\), so \(u(1/2, 1/2) = 1/4 (f_{00} + f_{10} + f_{11} + f_{01})\).

    Proof

    Tangent of a level set

    Since \(\nabla F(p) \ne 0\), either \(F_y (p) \ne 0\) or \(F_x (p) \ne 0\).

    Case \(F_y (p) \ne 0\). Apply Implicit function theorem: there are \(I\), \(J\) and a \(C^1\) function \(\phi : I \to J\) with \(\phi(p_x) = p_y\) and \(L_c \cap (I \times J) = \lbrace (x, \phi(x)) : x \in I\rbrace\). Put \(N = I \times J\) and \(\gamma(x) = (x, \phi(x))\). Differentiating \(F(x, \phi(x)) = c\) by the chain rule gives \(F_x + F_y \phi' = 0\) at \(\gamma(x)\). By continuity of \(F_y\), we may shrink \(I\) and \(J\) so that \(F_y \ne 0\) on \(N\); then

    \[ \gamma'(x) = (1, \phi'(x)) = (1, -F_x / F_y) = 1 / F_y (F_y, -F_x), \]

    which is non-zero and parallel to \((F_y, -F_x)\) at \(\gamma(x)\).

    Case \(F_x (p) \ne 0\). Exchange the roles of \(x\) and \(y\): there is a \(C^1\) function \(\psi\) with \(L_c \cap N = \lbrace (\psi(y), y)\rbrace\), curve \(\gamma(y) = (\psi(y), y)\) and \(\psi' = -F_y / F_x\), so

    \[ \gamma'(y) = (-F_y / F_x, 1) = -1 / F_x (F_y, -F_x), \]

    again non-zero and parallel to \((F_y, -F_x)\).

    Finally \(\nabla F \cdot (F_y, -F_x) = F_x F_y - F_y F_x = 0\).

    Export

    Proof

    Rounding error

    The difference of the curves is

    \[ \widetilde{B}(t) - B(t) = \sum_{i=0}^n b_{i,n}(t) (\widetilde{P}_i - P_i). \]

    By Partition of unity, the weights \(b_{i,n}(t)\) are non-negative and sum to 1, so for each coordinate

    \[ |\widetilde{B}_k (t) - B_k (t)| \le \sum_{i=0}^n b_{i,n}(t) |\widetilde{P}_{i,k} - P_{i,k}| \le \epsilon. \]

    Since \(\left\lVert v \right\rVert_2 \le \sqrt{d} \left\lVert v \right\rVert_\infty\) for \(v \in \mathbb{R}^d\) (Section 2.2 of Golub (2013)), we get \(\left\lVert \widetilde{B}(t) - B(t) \right\rVert_2 \le \sqrt{d} \epsilon\).

    The exporters write each coordinate with Python's fixed-point format with \(p\) decimals, which gives the multiple of \(10^{-p}\) nearest to the coordinate; it differs from the coordinate by at most \(1/2 \cdot 10^{-p}\). The exporters also write a coordinate whose absolute value is less than \(1/2 \cdot 10^{-p}\) as \(0\), which changes it by that value, again at most \(1/2 \cdot 10^{-p}\).
